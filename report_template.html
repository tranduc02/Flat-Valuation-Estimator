What we found along the way

  • {{floor_premium_pct}} a floor. Compared only with flats in the same building, each floor up sold for about {{floor_premium_pct}} more, after adjusting every sale to the same price level ({{floor_premium_n}} sales in {{floor_premium_buildings}} buildings). {{chart_floor}}
  • A flat's own earlier sale, moved to today's prices with a repeat-sales index, was a strong guide: flats that had sold before were within 10% {{sold_before_share}} of the time ({{sold_before_n}} sales), against {{never_sold_share}} for flats with no earlier sale ({{never_sold_n}} sales).
  • Half the floor premium is bigger flats. Within the same building, each floor up sold for about 1.3% more, but comparing only flats with the same number of bedrooms the gap falls to 0.7% a floor (25,596 sales). Higher floors tend to have more bedrooms.
  • Light and view didn't show up. Counting the taller buildings around each flat, and between it and the harbour, using OpenStreetMap heights, moved the backtest from [ABL] 66.4% to 64.3% within 10%. That is within the noise, so it's not proof it hurts, but no gain either. One height per building, on today's map, is probably too coarse to see one flat's view.

What did each input add?

The same backtest, refitting the model as each group of inputs is added. With a test set this size, differences under about 5 percentage points may be noise.

{{ablation_table}}

The extra 34,000 sales from the NSW Valuer General moved the score most: 3.5 points, to 68.2%. It was the only step bigger than the noise on 283 sales, and it cut the median miss by about $4,000. A flat's own earlier sales and its building's sales came next. Nearby buildings, the API's dwelling types and the OpenStreetMap light-and-view counts did not measurably move this single test. The floor-plan inputs helped only slightly, but in all four test periods of our wider check, which is why we kept them.

What data did we use?

  • The supplied flats-city-of-sydney.csv ({{n_flats}} addresses: location, mesh block, unit number, bedrooms where known, flats in the building), flat-sales-city-of-sydney.csv, and the supplied homes and people per mesh block (2026 figures are Microburbs estimates, not Census results).
  • NSW Valuer General bulk sales. The supplied sales turned out to be a subset of the state's sales records (same dates, same prices). Matching the Valuer General's unit sales to addresses added {{vg_sales_added}} sales the supplied file doesn't have, and the strata lot area (flat size) for {{vg_area_n}} flats. The final model learned from {{n_train_final}} sales in all.
  • OpenStreetMap buildings and stations. Building heights (where mapped, otherwise our own buildings' top floor) give a building height for {{osm_height_share}} of flats, so we can count taller buildings next to each flat and between it and the harbour. Stations give distance to train, metro and light rail.
  • Microburbs API. Property type and dwelling type (apartment, unit, studio, commercial...) for almost every address from /v1/suburbs/{suburb}/properties, and the floor where it gives one. Floor otherwise comes from the unit number (315 is floor 3); where both exist they agreed on all 19,570 flats. We skipped the API's bedrooms and parking, which carry no as-at date.
  • Only information dated by 31 August 2026. Sales after that date were removed before anything else ran, and only Valuer General files released by 31 August were read (later ones hold the judges' test period). In the backtest a Valuer General sale only counts once it had been registered. We did not use current bedrooms and parking from /basics/all, which carry no as-at date.

Value on 31 Aug 2026
  1. Price index. Flats that sold twice show how prices moved between the two dates. We fitted a smoothed quarterly index from those pairs and used it to move every past sale to a common price level.
  2. Comparable history. For each flat we took the index-adjusted prices of earlier sales of the same flat, the same building (and same bedroom count), the same floor plan on other floors (units 305, 405, 505... sit above each other and usually share a layout), the same mesh block, the same suburb and bedroom count, and the eight nearest other buildings with sales. Training rows only see sales before their own date, so the model learns exactly what it can use on 31 August.
  3. Flat details. Floor (from the unit number), position in the building, bedrooms, bathrooms, parking, building size, strata lot area, distance to the harbour and to Town Hall, homes and people in the mesh block.
  4. Light and view. A flat's height is its floor × 3.1 m. We count the taller buildings within 100 m, how far the tallest rises above the flat, and the taller buildings within 250 m in a 60° cone towards the nearest point of the harbour.
  5. Model. LightGBM with a median (L1) loss on log prices, so it targets percentage error, which is what the within-10% score rewards. Recent sales count more (four-year half-life). We train three copies with different random seeds and average them, which steadies the estimate.

The clever step: finding the missing sales. The supplied sales were a subset of the NSW Valuer General's public records. Matching the Valuer General's unit sales to addresses added 34,375 sales (42% of what the final model learned from), mostly earlier sales in the same building with the same bedroom count, the input the model relies on most. Reading only files released by 31 August keeps it inside the rules.

What remains uncertain?

  • Bedrooms are blank for most addresses, and those that exist come from the last advert, which may be dated after 31 August.
  • The model does not see condition, renovation or the direction a flat faces: units in a building usually share one map point. Our light-and-view counts use one height per building, and OpenStreetMap has heights for under half of buildings; it is also today's map, so a tower finished after an old sale still counts for that sale.
  • Strata lot area is only known for flats the Valuer General recorded an area for, and may include a car space or storage cage on the same lot.
  • {{no_bld_sentence}}
  • Cheap flats, and small flats high up in towers. Under $600,000 only 36% of estimates were within 10% (44 sales), against 81% between $600,000 and $800,000 (100 sales). Some of the worst misses were small flats high up in city towers, such as a one-bedroom on level 18 in Haymarket that sold for $570,000 and we valued at $809,000: the model gives them the building's and the floor's premium without knowing they are small. When we miss, we usually guess too high (70 of 91 misses).
